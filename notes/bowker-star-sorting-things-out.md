# Bowker and Star, "Sorting Things Out" (1999)

**What it is**
An ethnographic study of classification systems and information infrastructure, and the deepest criticism of ontology work in this corpus. Bowker and Star treat classification itself as an object of study, and argue that values, policies, and modes of practice become embedded in large information systems and get expressed as categories. Their central concept is *torque*: the twisting that happens when a classification system and a person's own account of themselves do not line up, and the system wins.

**Key concepts**
- **The International Classification of Diseases.** A seemingly neutral list took decades to negotiate and is still disputed. Tropical countries argue tropical diseases are under-represented against rich-world diseases. In Japan, where a heart attack is a low-status death, death certificates often record a stroke, skewing international comparison.
- **Apartheid race classification in South Africa.** People were reclassified between categories by government declaration, which forced changes of residence, job, and family. The category was not a description of a person. It was an instrument acting on one.
- **Tuberculosis patients**, wholly dependent on a doctor's diagnosis for their own status.
- **The Nursing Interventions Classification.** Nurses were asked to describe their work so it could be standardised and billed. Some welcomed becoming visible in the system. Others saw the classification constrain what they were free to do. The tension between the standard and the local adaptations needed to make it work in practice is the book's recurring finding.
- **Black-boxing.** Classifications become invisible infrastructure, and the effort and politics that produced them are forgotten.

**How you'd use it**
Use torque as a review question on any operational ontology: when a real case does not fit a category, who bears the cost? If the answer is a person outside the room, the modelling decision is a policy decision and should be made accordingly. Use the residual-category test as well: every model needs an "other" bucket, and what sits in it, and who put it there, tells you what the model is actually for.

**Pitfalls & lessons**
The book's argument is not that classification is avoidable. It is that classification is consequential and usually invisible, so the only real choice is whether the consequences are examined. One reviewer notes the book piles up examples without establishing which classification characteristics produce which effects, and that it is oddly disconnected from the empirical cognitive-categorisation literature. Take the case studies as demonstrations rather than as a predictive theory.
