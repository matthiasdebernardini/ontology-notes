# Shirky, "Ontology Is Overrated" (2005)

**What it is**
Clay Shirky's argument, assembled from two 2005 talks, that designing categories to cover possible cases in advance is badly overrated in digital systems. He is precise about his target: not classification in general, but *ontological* classification, which organises entities by their essences and possible relations, and assumes a new item's logical place already exists before the item does.

**Key concepts**
- **The periodic table as the best case.** It organises by proton count and gets enormous predictive value. Even so, "noble gas" is a frozen accident: helium is no more essentially a gas than mercury is essentially a liquid, but the chemists could not get it cold enough to see that. If a near-perfect scheme over physical essences still has context errors, softer domains have no chance.
- **Library catalogues optimise for shelves, not concepts.** Dewey's 200s give nine categories to Christianity and one to "other religions." Library of Congress History has DA Great Britain, DC France, and also DS Asia and DT Africa as co-equal top-level entries. What is being optimised is books on a shelf. "The essence of a book isn't the ideas it contains. The essence of a book is 'book.'"
- **There is no shelf.** Digital items have no physical constraint forcing one item into one place, so the constraint that produced hierarchical classification is gone.
- **The parable of the ontologist.** Yahoo, free of physical constraints, hired a professional ontologist and *added the shelf back*: Books and Literature appears in Entertainment with an "@" marking that it is not really there. Yahoo chose to assert what is real rather than let items sit in many places at once.
- **The alternative.** Links and tags. Free-form labelling looks like a recipe for disaster, but large messy data sets yield surprising value.

**How you'd use it**
Apply Shirky's three preconditions in reverse as a go/no-go test. Formal classification pays off when the domain has a small, stable, well-defined corpus with clear edges, when experts do the cataloguing, when those experts are authoritative and coordinated, and when users are trained. Genomics, museum records, and financial instruments qualify. General web content, user-generated media, and unbounded enterprise data usually do not.

**Pitfalls & lessons**
The essay is regularly overread as "ontologies are useless." Shirky attacks *a priori* categorisation applied to unbounded, uncoordinated, user-facing corpora. He does not deny that the periodic table works. Semantic Arts published a direct rebuttal to his companion essay on syllogism, and the disagreement about how much inference is worth doing is still live.
